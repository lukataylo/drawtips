Basic Forms

9 minute read · Needs: pencil, paper, one household object (a mug is perfect)

Look around the room. Every object you can see — the mug, the lamp, the chair, your own hand — can be broken down into combinations of just four simple forms: the sphere, the box, the cylinder, and the cone. This idea is called construction, and it's how nearly all professional artists actually draw.

A mug is a cylinder with a bent cylinder handle. A tree is a cone or sphere of foliage on a cylinder trunk. A head is a sphere with a box jaw. Once you can draw the four forms convincingly from any angle, you can construct anything — including things that don't exist. This is the skill that separates "copying pictures" from real drawing.

Shapes vs. forms

A shape is flat: circle, square, triangle. A form is solid: sphere, box, cone. The difference on paper is that a form is drawn as if it has a far side you could touch. Beginners draw shapes and wonder why their drawings look flat; artists draw forms and their drawings feel three-dimensional even as rough sketches.

The trick to thinking in forms is to draw through: sketch the hidden back edges of every form lightly, as if the object were made of glass. When you draw a cylinder, draw the full ellipse at the bottom, not just the front curve. When you draw a box, sketch the three hidden edges. Nobody will see them in the final drawing — but your brain will, and that's what makes the visible lines land in the right place.

Meet the four forms

The sphere

A circle becomes a sphere the moment you wrap a contour line around it (see the dashed ellipses above). Practice drawing circles with your whole arm, then wrap one horizontal and one vertical ellipse around each — suddenly it has a front and a back.

The box

The most useful form of the four, because almost everything fits inside one. Start with the front face, then push the depth edges back — all three depth edges should head in roughly the same direction and gently converge (more on why in Lesson 5).

The cylinder

Two ellipses connected by two straight lines. The classic beginner mistake is giving the top and bottom ellipses the same roundness — in reality the ellipse farther from your eye level is rounder. Look at a real mug from above, then slowly raise it to eye level and watch its opening flatten into a line.

The cone

A cylinder that tapers to a point. Same rules, one less ellipse.

Draw lightly. Construction lines should whisper. Keep your first pass so light you can barely see it, then commit with darker lines once the forms look right. Pressing hard too early is how drawings get locked into their mistakes.

Construction in action

Pick up your mug and really look at it. It's a large cylinder; the wall has thickness (a second, smaller ellipse inside the rim); the handle is a flattened, bent cylinder attached at two points. To draw it: light cylinder → inner rim ellipse → handle forms → then darken the lines that actually exist. You're not "drawing a mug" — you're assembling forms and then dressing them. Every complex object works exactly this way.

Exercise: form rotations

15 minutes. Ghost every line (Lesson 2). Print Worksheet 3 if you want guides.

  1. Boxes (7 min): fill half a page with boxes floating at different angles — long ones, flat ones, tilted ones. Draw through: all twelve edges every time, hidden ones lightly.
  2. Cylinders & cones (5 min): a row of cylinders standing, lying, and leaning. Full ellipses, both ends. Then three cones.
  3. Construct one object (3 min): draw your mug (or any simple object) using only forms drawn through, then darken the real edges.

Wonky is expected. The goal is not pretty boxes — it's teaching your brain to believe in the far side of every form. That belief is what "3D-looking drawing" is.
