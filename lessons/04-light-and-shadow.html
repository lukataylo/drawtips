Light & Shadow

9 minute read · Needs: a soft pencil (2B or softer), paper, a lamp and any round object

Everything you see, you see because of light. There are no outlines in the real world — just areas of light and shadow meeting each other. So once you can draw forms (Lesson 3), shading is what makes them real: it tells the viewer where the light is, how solid the object is, and even what it's made of.

The best part: light follows rules. You don't have to guess where shadows go — you can work them out. Learn the pattern once on a sphere, and you'll recognise it on faces, fruit, cars and clouds forever.

The five elements of shading

Put a ball on a table with one light source to its upper left. Look closely and you'll find five distinct zones:

  1. Highlight — the brightest spot, where light bounces straight into your eye. On paper: leave it untouched white.
  2. Light (halftone) — the surface facing the light, gradually darkening as it turns away.
  3. Core shadow — the darkest band on the object itself, where the surface has fully turned from the light. Note: it's a band, not the edge — because of…
  4. Reflected light — light bouncing off the table back onto the shadowed underside. It's what makes the bottom edge slightly lighter than the core shadow. This subtle zone is the #1 thing that makes shading look professional.
  5. Cast shadow — the shadow the object throws onto the table: darkest and sharpest right under the object, softer and lighter as it stretches away. It's what anchors the object to the ground instead of floating.

The big beginner mistake: shading the object's outline darkest and fading inward. Light doesn't work that way. Find the core shadow band, keep the reflected light at the edge, and never outline-shade again.

How to actually apply pencil

Use the overhand grip with the side of the lead, and build tone in layers: light, even passes, letting darkness accumulate gradually. Keep your strokes close together and consistent in direction, or gently follow the form around its curve. Squint at your subject constantly — squinting blurs detail and shows you the true pattern of light and dark.

Before shading any object, make a value scale: a strip of five boxes from untouched white to the darkest your pencil can go. This is your palette. Most beginner shading lives timidly in the lightest two values — force yourself to use all five and your drawings will instantly gain punch.

Exercise: the classic sphere

This is the most famous exercise in art education, because it works. 15–20 minutes.

  1. Draw a five-step value scale and fill it in, white to black.
  2. Set up a real light: one lamp, upper left, shining on any round object (orange, egg, ball). Other lights off.
  3. Draw a circle (arm swing, Lesson 2). Lightly mark where each of the five zones falls on your object — look, don't guess.
  4. Shade in layers: halftones first, then the core shadow band, then the cast shadow. Preserve the highlight and the reflected light.
  5. Final pass: squint at the object, then at your drawing. Darken whatever is too timid — it's almost always the core and cast shadows.

Repeat this exercise three times this week with the light in different positions. By the third sphere you'll be seeing the five elements on everything around you — that's the lesson doing its job.
